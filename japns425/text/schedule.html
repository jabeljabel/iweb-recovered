Schedule

Week 1.

27 August. Class introduction

29 August. Post-war Japanese prints.

Readings:

Donald Richie, “The Occupied Arts,” in The Confusion Era, 11-21A

Ian Littleton, The Idea of Japan, 70-75 A

Alicia Volk, Made in Japan: The Postwar Creative Print movement, 5-21, 45 A

Textures of Influence: 20th-Century Japanese Prints in the Collection of the University of

Maryland University College, 6-7, 13, 16, 18-20, 30-33, 50-54, 70, 114, 131 A

Oliver Statler, Modern Japanese Prints, xv-xvii, 3-8 A

Week 2.

3 September. Geo-politics of post-war Japan.

Readings:

“Basic Initial Post-Surrender Directive” (1945) A

Harry Emerson Wildes, “The War for the Mind of Japan” (1954) A

John Dower, “The Eye of the Beholder” (1969), followed by the excerpt from a 1951 National

Security Council Memorandum, 17 May 1951 A

Christine Klein, Cold War Orientalism (2003), 19-60, 117-35 A

4 September. First day to buy tickets for the bonus Eisenhower Auditorium events at a discount connected to this class (see November 12 for details on the performance related to this class, but the discount seems to apply to any performance you want to see; email with link will be forwarded to you through Angel).

5 September. Post-war Japanese ceramics.

CLASS MEETS IN CERAMICS GALLERY OF PALMER MUSEUM OF ART

Readings:

Bernard Leach, A Potter’s Book (1940), introduction by Soyetsu YANAGI and

Chapter 1 “Towards a Standard,” xvii-xxi, 1-27

Kenneth Beittel, Zen and the Art of Pottery, Chaps. 1 and 7 (*?) A*

Students assigned to small groups responsible for a set of objects: a woodblock print, a ceramic piece, and/or an illustrated book. In class and over the weekend, groups to confer and divide tasks following guidelines on task-sheet.

Week 3.

10 September. Japan from an American perspective.

Readings:

Ruth Benedict, The Chrysanthemum and the Sword (1946), forward, Ch.1, Ch. 2, Ch. 13

Bryan Victoria, Zen at War, 166-187 A*

Harry Harootunian, “Postwar America and the Aura of Asia” in The Third Mind: American

Artists Contemplate Asia, 1860-1989 (2009), 45-54 A*

Due: Task sheets from each group.

12 September. Japan from a Japanese perspective.

Readings:

John Dower, Embracing Defeat, Chaps. 3 and 4 (*A)

SAKAGUCHI Ango, “On Decadence”

Week 4

16 September. Extra credit opportunity. Michael Bourdagh speaks [about the author SOSEKI Natsume]; for extra credit, attend and write one paragraph tying any aspect of his talk to any aspect of our class reading and discussion to date.

17 September. American interventions.

Michael Bourdaghs in class

Readings:

F.S. Saunders “Modern Art was CIA ‘Weapon’ (1995) A

articles from Encounters [CIA-funded magazine]: Geoffrey Gorer, “Japan’s Past;”

 Edward Seidensticker, “On Trying to Translate Japanese;” Dazai, “Two Stories”

19 September. Encounter assignment.

Each group chooses two articles (not those assigned Tuesday!) from Encounter during the 1950s or 1960s accessible at: http://www.unz.org/Pub/Encounter

Be prepared to present your articles to the class: see Encounter assignment sheet for details.

Week 5

24 September. An oft-told tale.

Reading: KAWABATA Yasunari, “Izu Dancer” (written 1926, abridged translation 1955) A

26 September

Students present preliminary reports on their objects/labels

Week 6

1 October. Preliminary reports continue.

3 October. Visiting artist in class.

Week 7

8 October. Aestheticizing Japaneseness.

Reading: TANIZAKI Junchiro, In Praise of Shadows (1933)

10 October. Aestheticizing Japanese War.

Mayu Tsuruya, “Socialist Realism in the War Art of Imperial Japan,” in Art and War in Japan and its

 Empire, 1931-1960, 59-75 A

Kendall H. Brown, “Out of the Dark Valley: Japanese Woodblock Prints and War, 1937-1945,”

 in Art and War in Japan and its Empire, 1931-1960, 109-227

Week 8

14 October. Bert Winther Tamaki speaks; please arrange to attend this event outside of class time, but arranged for this class. Extra credit for engagement if you ask a question following the talk.

15 October. Discussion: Winther-Tamaki talk, projects, papers [start reading for Thursday]

17 October. Fictionalizing our Focus (yes, this is the novel about everything we’ve studied).

Kazuo ISHIGURO, An Artist of the Floating World

Week 9

22 October

Mid-term paper due: In a 6-7 page paper, develop and interesting, original thesis relating An Artist of Floating World with at least two other texts assigned for this class and two works of art (these can come from the Penn State collections, your research for the exhibition, or illustrations in class readings). These are minimum requirements; more specific references to class materials are welcome. Note requirements for papers given at the top of the syllabus.

In class: visiting artist.

24 October. Further Fictions of the Post-war Period.

Readings:

KOJIMA Nobuo, “American School” (1954)

TAMURA Taijiro, “Gates of Flesh” [*how long?]

James MICHENER, “Lobeck, the Asiatic”

Week 10.

29 October. Sayonara

Reading:

James MICHENER, Sayonara (1953)

31 October. Label theory.

Assignment: Go to the Palmer Museum of Art. Bring a camera. Photograph a discursive label (that is one that gives more than the bare-bones information about the object on display) that you find interesting, either because it serves as a model for what you want to accomplish with your label, or because it misses the point somehow. Submit your label to the dropbox on Angel by midnight on 30 October, and be prepared to describe in class why you chose it. (If you are looking ahead and want to use a different museum for this assignment, that would be great to give us some variety).

Week 11

5 November. in class labels workshop; bring what you consider polished drafts of your label(s)

7 November. Draft label copy due

in class: discuss film of Sayonara (147 minutes)

Week 12

12 November. The Japanese Inn

Reading:

Oliver Statler, The Japanese Inn, Chaps 1, Ch 4, Ch 14, Ch 14 A*

Extra credit opportunity. Yamato Drummers 7:30 PM Eisenhower Auditorium. We have arranged (hugely) discounted tickets for you ($12.45), which you can purchase at https://oss.ticketmaster.com/html/go.htmI?l=EN&t=psuarts&o=2993295&g=72; use code ENG 404 for extra credit, attend and write one paragraph tying any aspect of this performance to any class reading to date, including at least one quotation from that reading.

14 November. Mishima and the Temple of the Golden Pavilion

Readings:

Yukio MISHIMA Temple of the Golden Pavilion Chapters 1-5

“Mishima’s Way” and “Hanjo” from Encounter A

Week 13

19 November.

Readings:

Yukio MISHIMA Temple of the Golden Pavilion Chapters 5-10

Review from Encounter “Zen Arson” A

21 November. Theorizing and historicizing.

Kojin KARATANI, “Uses of Aesthetics: After Orientalism” (1998) A

[two notes about this reading. First, if you are not familiar with Edward Said’s influential book Orientalism, you can find the first 9 pages helpfully excerpted and illustrated – Said himself did not discuss art – in the readings file on the Angel site (this file comes from an open source for a University of California Sacramento course). Second, Karatani, himself is a very influential scholar, and this reading is key to making sense of the big ideas in this course, and will, therefore, feature in the final. If you were considering leaving scandalously early for Thanksgiving… think again!]

Thanksgiving break

Readings:

Week 15. Japanese Cultures in the World—past, present, and future

10 December, Anime, Gaming, and Edo.

Readings:

MURAKAMI Takashi, “Superflat Manifesto” *A

12 December

The Question of Art After 3-11: Representational Crisis and Pop Culture

Readings:

Toshinao SASAKI, “Post-Disaster Reconstruction, Gov 2.0 and the Platformizing World”

Kentaro TAKEKUMA, “The Day The “Endless Everyday” Ended”

FINAL PAPER (due at finals time assigned to this class): In a 6-7 page paper, develop and interesting, original thesis relating to the second half of the course. Papers must include at least one novel (Sayonara, Temple of the Golden Pavilion, or The Old Capital), Karatani’s “Uses of the Aesthetic,” and least two more assigned texts (these can include another second novel) from any point in the class, and one work of art (from the Penn State collections, your research for the exhibition, or illustrations in class readings). These are minimum requirements; more specific references to class materials are welcome.Note requirements for papers given at the top of the syllabus.
