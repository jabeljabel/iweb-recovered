Jonathan E. Abel
 
Tracing Censorship Through the Archive
The image above is a cover of the August 1927 issue of Bungei shijō (here with the variant title Bungei ichiba, Arts Market) depicting a map of the tortuous path a publication must follow only to be banned.  With the sensational explanatory notes titled “Secrets Revealed: the Road to Magazine Bans under the Newpaper Act,” the map depicts how a galley travels via post, telegraph, and truck through various levels of the bureaucracy—revealing presumably the irrationality, waste, and irritation involved in attempting to get something past the censors.  Following the arrows across the front and back covers the reader sees, for instance, that one might even encounter a government minister in receiving a ban.  The minister is depicted as sitting back smoking a cigar.
 
My current book project, “Archiving Censors: The Preservation and Production of Banned Japanese Discourse, 1923-1970,” centers on the curious interaction between archives and censors.  On the one hand, censors lock away texts deemed dangerous or offensive to society.  On the other hand, their acts of suppression yield an archive of writing on, against, and in favor of censorship, to be passed down to posterity.  Rather than destroying, censors collect and preserve what they deemed impermissible—a fundamentally contradictory function attested to by the extant archive of the Japanese imperial censor (1923-1945) and the archive of the American Occupation censors (1945-1952).  Meanwhile some authors and publishers, faced with a censorship that seeks to remove specific words, topics, and entire genres from circulation, have risked bans through intentional use of the authorities’ definitions of offense, recasting the censor’s categories as a veritable code of the obscene that can increase sales.  In these ways, censors and producers alike archive or leave legible traces of censorship.  I read these multiple archives of banned literature as groups of marginalized works that can be brought into the realm of the readable, even while they act as collections of clues suggesting the contours of the unwritable. Using an array of historical sources—including censors’ examination copies, political cartoons, and literary manuscripts—I argue that the study of literary suppression and subaltern literatures must be accompanied by a recognition of criticisms’ own inherent and constant construction of new margins.  We cannot disregard this constantly volatile boundary in our necessary pursuit of righting wrongs done to the subaltern.
