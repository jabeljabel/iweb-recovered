Friday, February 15, 2013

Jonathan Abel REDACTED The archives [...]

ALEXANDER JACOBY

Published: 15 February 2013

Jonathan Abel REDACTED The archives of censorship in transwar Japan 376pp. University of California Press. $65; distributed in the UK by Wiley. £44.95.

978 0 520 27334 4 This original and important book focuses on the mechanics of censorship in Japan in the second quarter of the twentieth century. (The subtitle, with its reference to "transwar Japan", highlights what Jonathan Abel perceives as "continuities between the two censorship systems" of militarist and Occupation-era Japan. This is a useful corrective to assumptions that 1945 marked a clean break.) Abel analyses not only censored texts, but the practices and perceptions of the censors, and the way in which library and archival policies themselves shape, and to an extent distort, our understanding of past censorship decisions. Several chapters focus on the redaction marks used both by censors and cautious editors to obscure subversive words and topics, showing how they draw attention to the act of suppression and how readers sought to decipher them.

Redacted adopts a libertarian perspective. Censorship, in a repeated metaphor, "wounds discourses"; redaction marks are "scars"; censorship, Abel contends, does lasting violence.

One cannot simply redress the balance by reprinting forbidden texts, which may be counterproductive insofar as it obscures the historical record, allowing modern readers to assume that texts were available when they were in fact censored or banned.

Paraphrasing Heinrich Heine, Abel argues that "there is no wounding of texts without also wounds to bodies". One is reluctant to question this emphasis on the negative consequences of censorship, when censorship is undoubtedly a repressive act. But it is also possible to see censorship as one of the conditions of production, as are social attitudes, stylistic conventions and so forth - circumstances to which authors and artists responded creatively. For instance, the flowering of the period film in 1930s Japan was, in part, a response to the difficulty of directly addressing the political troubles of the present. The films of Sadao Yamanaka would have been different if made under a more liberal regime, but not necessarily better. The same may be said of much art produced in repressive circumstances, from the historical novels of Jaan Kross in Soviet Estonia to the films of Abbas Kiarostami in theocratic Iran.

For Abel, perhaps, this would be an example of what he condemns as "the crass liberal portrayal of the scene of censorship". In any case, it is not an objection that challenges the formidable scholarship and intelligence of this book. Abel breaks new ground, and largely achieves his goal of crafting an ethical response to the moral questions posed by censorship.
