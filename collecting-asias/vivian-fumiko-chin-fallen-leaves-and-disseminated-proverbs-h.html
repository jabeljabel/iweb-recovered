Vivian Fumiko Chin, “Fallen Leaves and Disseminated Proverbs: Hoarding, Loss, and Reinvention in Asian Collections and Recollections”

Saturday, September 28, 2013

Fortune cookie aphorisms; slogans from The Little Red Book; racist jokes; Chinese/English dictionary apps; mildewy titles like, Selected Chinese Sayings, Proverbs and Common Sayings from the Chinese, The Four Books: With English Translation and Notes; advertising; memory; and Asian Diasporic Literature â€“ these sources provide a wellspring of collections of make-believe, historic, and everyday Chinese and Asian proverbial sayings.  What can we make of such a collection? This discussion addresses the practice of collecting idiomatic sayings and the use of proverbs in everyday life and in literature. Growing up frequently admonished that, “every grain of rice is a drop of sweat” may render it impossible to leave food on one’s plate. Thus, such idioms become ingrained in the body, dictating our daily habits. In addition, I argue that the use of proverbs and quasi-proverbs in Asian diasporic work ranging from that of Quebecois Chinese author Ying Chen, Vietnamese Australian writer Nam Le, Chinese American writers Fae Myenne Ng and Yiyun Li, provide evidence of the enduring nature of idiom. For those whose command of an Asian language may be minimal, the proverb serves as a way to both maintain and reinvent a cultural epistemology. While one may lack fluency, one may nonetheless apply a small byte of a language to current concerns involving the loss and reinvention of tradition, questions of authenticity, global capitalism, national identity, and diasporic issues in ways that demonstrate how fallen leaves both return to the root and grow new roots.  In this practice, Asian collections do not gather dust but are revived and put to use in everyday life.

 

Associate Professor, Mills College

 

Vivian Fumiko Chin
