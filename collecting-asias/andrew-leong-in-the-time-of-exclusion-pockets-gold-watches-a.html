Andrew Leong, “In the Time of Exclusion: Pockets, Gold, Watches and (Japanese) Japanese American Literature”

Saturday, September 28, 2013

 

Strangely enough, the American immigration law sometimes known as the “Asian Exclusion Act of 1924” was not known as such during its inception or twenty-eight years of enforcement. However, during the 1960s, what was once usually known as the “Oriental” Exclusion Act gradually became more and more “Asian.” By the early 2000s, the nominally inaccurate but conceptually evocative idea of the “Asian Exclusion Act” had achieved such currency that the phrase entitled a powerful polemic decrying the disciplinary “exclusion acts” between Asian and Asian American studies. In sum, the odd temporality of the Asian Exclusion Act raises the question of how a law which, in original effect, only restricted the immigration of Japanese imperial subjects, came to be understood as a collective exclusion of all “Orientals,” “Asiatics,” or “Asians.”

       In this paper, I explore how exclusion works as a legal and narrative mode that describes not only a space but also a form of collective time. I perform a comparative analysis of temporal figures in two works of literature written in the aftermath of two distinct exclusions, the “Asian” Exclusion Act of 1924, and the Civil Exclusion Orders of 1942. The first work, Nagahara ShÅson’s Japanese-language novel, Lament in the Night (Yoru ni nageku, 1925), was originally written and published in Los Angeles. The second work, Momoko Iko’s English-language play, Gold Watch (1970), was awarded one of the first prizes in Asian American playwriting, and first performed in Los Angeles in 1972.

       Although ShÅson’s and Iko’s works are radically separated by genre, generation, and language, through uncanny coincidence, the action of both narratives turns on the same fetish object: a gold pocket watch. Through readings of these coincidental watches, I argue that “exclusion” is a central temporal figure for any understanding of a collective “Asian” or “Asian American” time, even if this figure is always at risk of collapsing into the realm of a “collector’s fetish.”

 

Andrew Leong (AB, Dartmouth College; PhD, University of California, Berkeley) is a comparativist who works primarily in English and Japanese, with additional interests in Spanish and Portuguese.

Leong has taught courses spanning a range of textual and visual media—from nineteenth and twentieth-century Japanese and American literature; to comics and manga; to Westerns, film noir, and Japanese period drama. He is also a translator of Japanese vernacular literature written and published in the Americas. His translations of two novels by Nagahara Shoson—who wrote for a Japanese reading public in Los Angeles during the 1920s—have been collected in a single volume: Lament in the Night (Kaya, 2012). Leong's current translation projects include a three-act play by Nagahara entitled The Ones Who Leave (去り行く者, 1928), as well as a collection of three novellas by Tamura Shogyo entitled Flowers of North America (北米の花, 1909).

Leong is currently working on a book, The City of the Migrant, which recovers the writings of Japanese immigrants and sojourners who lived and worked in the United States during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Through readings of works by Sadakichi Hartmann, Yone Noguchi, Arishima Takeo, Nagai Kafu, and others, Leong argues that Japanese men barred from the full rights and privileges of political citizenship staked claims to a literary citizenship forged through bonds of male homosocial friendship.
