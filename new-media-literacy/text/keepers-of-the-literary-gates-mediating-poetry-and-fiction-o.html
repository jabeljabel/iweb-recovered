“Keepers of the Literary Gates – Mediating Poetry and Fiction on the Chinese Internet”

Heather Inwood is Assistant Professor of modern Chinese cultural studies in the Department of East Asian Languages and Literatures.  Her teaching and research interests include Chinese popular fiction, internet culture (especially spoofing, online fiction, and popular poetry movements), and constructions of “Chineseness” in pop music from the PRC, Hong Kong, and Taiwan.  She teaches an undergraduate course, China Pop! Contemporary Popular Culture and Media in Greater China, once a year, and regularly offers graduate seminars in Chinese internet culture and recent Chinese popular fiction.  Her entry on the popular culture of East and Southeast Asia, 1900 to the present, appears in Cultural Sociology of the Middle East, Asia, and Africa, published by SAGE, 2012.

“Keepers of the Literary Gates – Mediating Poetry and Fiction on the Chinese Internet”

“Keepers of the Literary Gates – Mediating Poetry and Fiction on the Chinese Internet”

Heather Inwood (Ohio State University)

This paper explores processes of literary gatekeeping on the mainland Chinese internet by focusing on three case studies: avant-garde poetry produced within tightknit online communities, poems that “go viral” through mass participation, and popular fiction published on commercial literary websites.  I suggest that the concept of gatekeeping can help shed light on some of the key developments to have occurred within Chinese literature in the age of the internet.  These include, in poetry, the growing influence of poets who “worship the low” (chongdi) at the expense of those who “worship the high” (chongdi) in the early-to-mid 2000s and several high-profile spoofings (egao) poetry between 2006 and 2011, and in popular fiction, the continual emergence of new genres such as tomb-robbing, time travel, and web-game fiction.  Gatekeepers are mediators who filter data on the basis of economic, ideological, and aesthetic considerations.  Existing research on internet filtering falls mostly within the fields of political science, information technology, and business studies, with a few cultural scholars examining viral marketing and cross-platform promotional campaigns.  In the case of the People’s Republic of China, researchers have overwhelmingly chosen to focus on politically motivated gatekeeping on the internet.  Such scholarship far outweighs research into the largely apolitical micro-processes that determine which individual creators and cultural texts are able to rise to the surface of what would otherwise be experienced as a vast sea of noise.  In this paper I propose three models for filtering or gatekeeping literature on the Chinese internet: the xianchang or "live scene" model, the crowd-based or viral model, and the spreadable media model.  My goal is to encourage discussion of how new media technologies, the people who employ them, and the social spaces they create are together shaping the public face of contemporary Chinese literature and culture.
